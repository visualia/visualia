# HARNO keynote: consolidated research and narrative synthesis

**Working title:** *Kellele sa seda teed?* (Who are you making it for?)  
**Event:** *Raamistikust klassiruumi*, 27 November 2026, Öpik Conference Centre, Tallinn  
**Format:** 35-minute closing keynote, 15-minute Q&A  
**Audience:** ~150 teachers, school leaders and educational technologists  
**Research cut-off:** 11 November 2026  
**Status:** Working synthesis of the preceding research rounds. Sources are linked where available; numerical claims and recent publications must be checked against originals before presentation. This is not a fully independently audited literature review.

## 1. The central insight

AI is making it dramatically easier for one person to produce software, images, text and complex systems. But production is not the same as understanding, learning, judgement, responsibility, livelihood or meaning.

The most compelling tension is this:

> **Just as young people gain unprecedented tools for making things, some of the traditional routes through which beginners become skilled, employed and connected to professional communities may be narrowing.**

The answer cannot simply be “teach everyone to code,” “teach everyone to be creative,” or “teach everyone to collaborate.” Curiosity, play and exploration have intrinsic value before a project has a purpose or audience. Solitude can be productive. Other people can enrich and redirect exploration, but not every experiment needs external validation.

The keynote can bring together four questions:

1. **What can I make?** Access to creative tools and new capabilities.
2. **What do I discover or understand by making it?** Learning, exploration and judgement.
3. **Who controls the tools and the process?** Agency, ownership, data and institutional power.
4. **Who can I learn with, and how can I enter a community of practice?** Listening, feedback, mentorship, responsibility and work.

The speaker's *Lonely Software* essay and Positron experiments are the narrative centre, not an example tacked onto educational theory. The keynote should be an exploration, not another framework delivered to an audience that has spent the day discussing frameworks.

## 2. Why this talk belongs at this conference

HARNO's updated digital competence model, informed by DigComp, already includes content creation, programming, collaboration, problem-solving, purposeful AI use, adaptation of technology and peer evaluation. It is **not** credible to argue that the organisers care only about passive, cautious technology use.

The more productive question is: **What can a competence framework describe, and what must be lived through experience, experimentation and relationships?**

The speaker is not an education researcher. His authority comes from decades of writing software (since 1988), building interactive systems, experimental music, AI-assisted creative work and current course design. Teachers already know the distinction between producing an impressive answer and actually learning something. The talk should recognise their expertise.

Relevant primary sources:
- [HARNO learner digital competence assessment criteria](https://digipadevus.ee/oppija-digipadevusmudel/hindamiskriteeriumid/). Previously identified criteria include choosing whether to use AI (5.2), adapting AI to one's needs (5.2.9), designing improvements (5.3), evaluating one's own and others' work (5.3.4), and AI as a creative partner (5.3.5). **Verify exact wording and version for the 27 November launch.**
- [European Commission, DigComp 3.0](https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/scientific-activities/key-competences-lifelong-learning/digital-competence-framework-digcomp/digcomp-30_en).
- [European Commission, DigCompEdu](https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/digcompedu_en). Teacher agency and professional judgement are relevant to ongoing revision work; **check publication status and final wording**.

**Implication:** “Pupils should become makers, not just users” is already present in educational policy. The distinctive contribution is what happens *after* the power to make becomes abundant.

## 3. Creativity, curiosity and play: a longer history

The most relevant history is not primarily AI history. It is the history of humans using materials, rules, chance and feedback to discover things they could not specify in advance.

### Friedrich Fröbel: kindergarten and manipulable materials (nineteenth century)

Blocks, geometric forms, songs and hands-on play let children discover relationships by manipulating materials. The value of a block is not that it solves a predefined problem but that it invites experiments.

**Key connection:** A tool can be an invitation to investigate rather than a machine for completing tasks.

### John Dewey: *Art as Experience* (1934)

Making involves a reciprocal relationship between doing and undergoing: the maker acts, perceives what happens, and changes the next action. An idea can emerge through the encounter with material rather than exist fully formed beforehand.

**Key connection:** The Positron loopback can literalise feedback, but endless system variation is not the same as a meaningful human experience.

Background: [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Dewey's aesthetics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/dewey-aesthetics/).

### John Cage: chance and indeterminacy (1950s onward)

Chance procedures and indeterminate compositions allow outcomes the composer did not fully prescribe. Uncertainty is a creative resource rather than a defect.

**Key connection:** “What happens if I connect this to that?” can be a complete starting question. Not every experiment needs a product brief.

### Seymour Papert: Logo, *Mindstorms* (1980), constructionism

Computers can be materials to think *with*, not merely devices that deliver instruction. Children can investigate ideas by constructing, testing and revising personally meaningful artefacts. Constructionism must not be flattened into “any making equals learning.”

Read: [Papert and Idit Harel, *Situating Constructionism* (1991)](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~mjguz/csl/home.cc.gatech.edu/allison/uploads/4/papert91). See also Yasmin Kafai's work on children making games to teach mathematics to younger pupils.

**Key connection:** AI alters what children can build, but not the need for understanding, debugging, reflection and ownership.

### Arvo Pärt: tintinnabuli and productive constraints (1976)

Pärt's new compositional language emerged through sustained searching and severe formal constraints. More options do not automatically produce more creativity. Interpretation also requires performers and listeners.

Read: [Arvo Pärt Centre](https://www.arvopart.ee/en/).

**Key connection:** Freedom can mean choosing constraints, not maximising generated output. Do not equate Pärt's composition with children's play; use it as a distinct case of searching and listening.

### Reggio Emilia and Loris Malaguzzi: a hundred languages

Children investigate and express ideas through drawing, sound, movement, construction, conversation and other media. The atelier makes exploration and materials central.

Read: [Reggio Children, *The Hundred Languages of Children*](https://www.reggiochildren.it/en/rc/exhibitions/the-hundred-languages-of-children/).

**Key connection:** AI education should not collapse into text prompts. A learner's inquiry can move among sound, code, gesture and physical objects.

### Mitchel Resnick: *Lifelong Kindergarten* (2017)

Creative learning is a recurring spiral of **imagine → create → play → share → reflect → imagine again**, supported by projects, peers, passion and play. Play is not a reward for finishing serious work; it can be the means by which serious learning occurs.

Read: [MIT Media Lab, *Lifelong Kindergarten*](https://www.media.mit.edu/publications/lifelong-kindergarten/).

### Alison Gopnik: exploration versus exploitation

Exploration seeks information and possibilities without knowing which will pay off. Exploitation uses existing knowledge to reach a known goal efficiently. Childhood affords a protected period of broad exploration, partly because others provide care and support.

Read: [*Childhood as a Solution to Explore-Exploit Tensions* (2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32475327/). Prior research discussion also identified experimental work with Emily Liquin on children's exploratory behaviour; verify precise citation before quoting.

**Key connection:** AI products are often optimised to complete tasks quickly, while curiosity can begin before a task is even defined. A supportive community can protect exploration without demanding immediate usefulness.

### Margaret Boden: conceptual spaces and creativity

Boden distinguishes exploring a space of possibilities from transforming the rules that define it. This is useful for examining whether AI merely generates more options or helps us rethink what counts as possible.

Read: *The Creative Mind: Myths and Mechanisms* (1990; later editions).

### Synthesis

**Curiosity is valuable before we know what it will produce.** Play is not a decorative addition to productivity. The keynote should avoid treating creativity as worthwhile only when it serves someone else, generates a business, or prepares a child for employment.

## 4. Maggie Appleton, situated software and the social life of making

This is the strongest lineage connecting software abundance to the *Lonely Software* story.

### Clay Shirky, *Situated Software* (2004)

Small tools can be designed for particular people and social situations rather than anonymous markets. Their success may depend on shared context and relationships outside the application. Shirky discusses student projects, including a proposal involving a student's schoolteacher mother.

Read: [*Situated Software*](https://gwern.net/doc/technology/2004-03-30-shirky-situatedsoftware.html).

### Robin Sloan, *An App Can Be a Home-Cooked Meal* (2020)

Sloan's small family messaging app was built for a known group rather than growth. Software can be intimate, stable, and controlled by the people who use it. His later reflections emphasise sovereignty, not just ease of construction.

Read: [*An App Can Be a Home-Cooked Meal*](https://www.robinsloan.com/notes/home-cooked-app/) and [*Five Years of Home-Cooked Apps*](https://www.robinsloan.com/lab/five-years-of-home-cooked-apps/).

### Maggie Appleton, *Home-Cooked Software and Barefoot Developers* (2024)

AI may reduce the cost of building software for small, local and idiosyncratic needs. The barefoot developer need not be a professional programmer; their strength is knowledge of the people and circumstances around them. But personalised software may still depend on cloud platforms and infrastructure the maker cannot control.

Read: [Appleton, *Home-Cooked Software and Barefoot Developers*](https://maggieappleton.com/home-cooked-software).

### Maggie Appleton, *One Developer, Two Dozen Agents, Zero Alignment* (2026)

Coding agents accelerate implementation, but shared understanding, agreement, maintenance and responsibility do not accelerate automatically. People can receive completed implementations before they have agreed what should be built. Her collaborative-agent explorations aim to preserve shared context and participation.

Read: [Appleton, *One Developer, Two Dozen Agents, Zero Alignment*](https://maggieappleton.com/zero-alignment).

### The useful distinction

| Source | Core question | Missing condition |
|---|---|---|
| Shirky | Can software belong to a specific social situation? | Local context |
| Sloan | Can software be made and governed by its users? | Care and sovereignty |
| Appleton 2024 | Who gets to make software? | Access and capability |
| Appleton 2026 | How do we decide and build together? | Coordination and shared understanding |
| *Lonely Software* | Who is listening? | Meaningful encounter, interpretation, response |

Appleton is mostly concerned with improving software production and coordination. *Lonely Software* asks a less instrumental question: what happens when a person can continue creating entire worlds without needing anyone else?

**Do not force collaboration as the cure.** Private experiments, solitude and idiosyncratic tools matter. Distinguish **chosen solitude** from **enforced isolation**, and sharing as an invitation from sharing as a requirement.

## 5. The difficult labour-market question: who gets to become an expert?

This is the necessary counterweight to a joyful story about accessible creation.

### Evidence previously identified

- **Estonia:** OSKA figures cited in November 2026 reporting indicate that employment among ICT specialists under 25 fell from **2,405 in 2022 to 1,839 in 2025**, a decline of about **24%**. **Verify the underlying OSKA tables and the reporting before placing the figure on a slide.** It does not establish how much AI caused.
- **United States:** Stanford Digital Economy Lab's August 2026 update reports a **19% relative employment shortfall** for workers aged 22–25 in highly AI-exposed occupations compared with less-exposed peers. This is **not** a 19% decline in all junior software jobs and **not proof that AI caused the full difference**. The analysis points to reduced hiring as an important mechanism.
- **Junior vacancies:** A 2026 IZA study was previously reported as finding a **14–15% relative decline** in junior versus senior software developer vacancies, with increasing emphasis on problem-solving, communication and attention to detail. **Verify paper, identification strategy and estimates.**
- **Pipedrive:** October 2026 layoffs and management statements about AI were raised in earlier research. **Verify exact numbers, wording and attribution.** A company's explanation is not independent causal evidence.
- **Alternative pathway:** Some digital work may spread into healthcare, manufacturing, education, arts and other sectors. This is a plausible opportunity, not proof that new roles will compensate for lost entry-level jobs.

Sources to recheck: [Stanford Digital Economy Lab](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/), [OSKA](https://oska.kutsekoda.ee/), [Arenguseire Keskus](https://arenguseire.ee/), and the specific IZA working paper.

### The broken-apprenticeship hypothesis

Beginners traditionally learn by doing imperfect work, receiving criticism, watching experts, repeating tasks and gradually taking responsibility. If organisations automate much of the work once assigned to juniors, they may improve short-term productivity while reducing the opportunities that develop future experts.

The tension:

> **If we no longer need beginners to do the work, how will beginners ever become experts?**

This is a serious structural question for employers, universities and policymakers as well as schools. Do not transfer responsibility for labour-market disruption onto children or teachers. Do not promise that creativity alone guarantees employability.

### Historical reading for this problem

- **Michael Polanyi, *The Tacit Dimension* (1966):** Some knowledge is difficult to express as explicit rules and develops through practice.
- **Donald Schön, *The Reflective Practitioner* (1983):** Professional judgement develops through action, surprise and reflection in uncertain situations.
- **Jean Lave and Etienne Wenger, *Situated Learning: Legitimate Peripheral Participation* (1991):** Expertise involves gradually joining a community of practice, not merely accumulating individual skills.

These works have been identified as highly relevant, but their complete texts were not freshly reread in the prior rounds.

### How it connects to play

Play and curiosity are not a cheerful diversion from this problem. They help people learn to notice, investigate, persist, revise and respond to unfamiliar situations. But they cannot replace paid apprenticeships, mentorship or institutional responsibility.

The strongest juxtaposition for the talk is: **more creative power, fewer assured routes into expertise**.

## 6. What educational research says about AI and learning

The critical distinction is **performance versus learning**. A polished answer, generated lesson or working program does not establish durable understanding.

- [OECD, *Digital Education Outlook 2026*](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026_062a7394-en.html): Prior research review highlighted evidence that general-purpose AI can improve immediate task performance without guaranteeing lasting learning, whereas pedagogically designed systems can support learning. **Read the underlying chapters and cited studies before using strong claims.**
- [UNESCO, *Global Education Monitoring Report 2023: Technology in Education: A Tool on Whose Terms?*](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000385723): Educational technology should be judged by whose needs it serves, evidence of benefit, equity and control.
- **Liu and colleagues, October 2026 AI tutoring trial:** Earlier research reported a randomised university study involving 2,379 undergraduates and 30 instructors, with negative grade and engagement estimates for AI-tutor access in certain comparisons. [Working paper DOI](https://doi.org/10.26300/y3f8-vh05). **Recheck methods, comparison, effect sizes and publication status before citing.**
- **Contractor and Reyes, 2026:** Earlier research reported an experiment in which tutoring-oriented AI use produced more durable learning gains than delegating writing to AI. [Preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08849). **Preprint; verify details.**
- **Structured versus unrestricted AI tutoring:** Earlier research identified a small 2026 MBA study where structured AI tutoring outperformed unrestricted use and voice did not significantly improve learning. [Preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23958). **Small study; verify.**

These studies involve different populations, interventions and outcomes. They cannot be collapsed into “AI helps learning” or “AI harms learning.” The useful question is **what the learner continues to think, do, test and understand**.

For a teacher audience, this should be acknowledged rather than explained as a new discovery from the software industry.

## 8. Agency and ownership: more than choosing prompts

The experimental art-school course offers a concrete demonstration of choices that are usually hidden:

- Run open models locally where feasible.
- Observe time, energy and cost.
- Make inference happen when a student asks for it.
- Do not build continuous teacher dashboards of students' internal creative processes.

These choices raise a distinction between **an AI system designed to encourage learner autonomy** and **a learner who has meaningful control over the AI system itself**.

Questions worth exploring:

- Can I decide not to use AI?
- Can I change or replace the tool?
- What information does it collect, and who can inspect it?
- Who can alter its rules?
- Can the learner explain, challenge or refuse its suggestions?
- Who maintains it when the original creator leaves?

Do not imply that all learning analytics are surveillance or that local models guarantee privacy, quality or independence. Distinguish student-chosen disclosure, legitimate assessment, institutional requirements and vendor data collection.

Historical reference: **Ivan Illich, *Tools for Conviviality* (1973)**, plus his work on radical monopoly and learning webs. His argument concerns tools that support agency rather than organise people into dependency. It should not be reduced to “open source is always good.”

## 9. The live demonstration and the *Lonely Software* essay

The demonstration should show a real process of discovery, not merely how fast an AI agent generates software.

### Phase A: Curiosity

Start with a simple, legible system: sound, image, hardware or a patch. Connect its output back to its input. Change a parameter. Let the result surprise you. Explain the experiment as “I wanted to see what would happen,” not as a productivity achievement.

### Phase B: Abundance

Show how AI allows one person to assemble and modify systems at a speed that previously required much more time or a team. Allow genuine delight in that freedom.

### Phase C: The lonely node

The system can keep responding to itself. It can generate variation and feedback. But what is being learned, and by whom? The essay asks, **“But who is listening?”** This is an artistic and relational question, **not** a strict claim that AI cannot simulate listeners or feedback.

### Optional Phase D: Another participant

An audience member introduces an unexpected sound, movement or decision that genuinely changes the system. The purpose is not to prove that group work is always superior. It is to demonstrate that a new encounter can alter the direction of inquiry.

### Closing

Return to the essay's open ending: **“Just the possibility to see what happens next.”** Resist resolving the story with a moral instruction about collaboration, employment or useful software.

## 10. What the keynote should *not* say

- “Every pupil will become a programmer.” Not supported and not necessary.
- “AI has already caused all the decline in junior IT jobs.” Causation is uncertain.
- “Creativity will guarantee future employment.” It will not.
- “Children should make things for someone else.” Too instrumental; private play matters.
- “Collaboration solves loneliness.” Not necessarily.
- “The new digital competence framework ignores creation.” It does not.
- “Teachers need to discover that output is not learning.” They already know.
- “Learning analytics are inherently surveillance.” Design, governance and consent matter.
- “Faster production is always harmful.” It can also liberate time for exploration.
- “AI is only a threat.” The joy of new possibilities is essential to the story.

## 11. Revised 35-minute narrative arc

**0–8 min: The joy of making**  
A personal history from 1988 to today. Software as a material for exploration. A live patch or feedback experiment begins with curiosity, not a product requirement.

**8–15 min: A world that can make itself**  
AI-assisted creation and the astonishing new capacity of one person. *Lonely Software*: the loopback, abundance, and the unexpected question of who is listening.

**15–21 min: The contradiction for the young**  
Young people can make more, yet entry-level opportunities may be shrinking. Introduce the carefully qualified Estonian and Stanford data. Ask how beginners will become experts if beginner work disappears. No doom montage.

**21–28 min: What making teaches, and what it does not**  
Papert, Resnick and Gopnik, conveyed through a concrete experiment rather than a parade of names. Exploration, productive struggle, tacit knowledge and feedback. A polished result is not proof of learning.

**28–33 min: Tools we can shape, people we can meet**  
Appleton, home-cooked software and the art-school local-model course. Control over tools, shared context, chosen solitude and the option of meaningful participation. One actual Estonian classroom example, if verified.

**33–35 min: Return to curiosity**  
The live system and the original question. What do we want young people to remain able to explore, understand and change? End openly with the possibility of seeing what happens next.

This arc is a recommendation, not a locked script. The labour-market section could move earlier if the audience needs a stronger immediate stake, but avoid opening a tired 15:00 audience with a bleak statistical sequence.

## 12. Likely objections and grounded responses

### “Children already use AI to avoid thinking. Why encourage more making?”

Producing something and understanding it are different. The talk is not proposing that generated outputs replace foundational reading, writing, mathematics or reasoning. It is exploring when making becomes a way of thinking, testing and learning. Teachers are better placed than the speaker to judge what counts as learning in their classrooms.

### “Why must art or play be for someone?”

It need not be. Private exploration is valuable. Other people can expand what happens next, but external usefulness is not the condition of creativity.

### “Are you telling teachers to solve the youth employment crisis?”

No. Employers, universities and policymakers must preserve entry routes and mentorship. Schools should not be assigned responsibility for structural labour-market changes. The point is that opportunities to explore, make mistakes and gradually develop judgement matter, whether or not a particular job survives.

### “Our framework already includes creativity and agency.”

Yes. The talk is not replacing it. It makes the lived experience of those words tangible, including their unresolved tensions.

### “Not every school can run local models or maintain experimental hardware.”

They should not have to. The technical demonstration illustrates agency, not a procurement recommendation. The underlying questions can be explored with paper, classroom artefacts, ordinary tools and peer feedback.

## 13. One Monday activity: someone else tries it

**Time:** 10 minutes. **Technology:** None required.

A pupil takes something they have already created: a mathematical explanation, story, drawing, presentation, experiment or program. Another pupil encounters it without the creator explaining it first and answers:

1. What did you understand?
2. Where did you need help, or what would you change?

The creator chooses whether to revise and explains the decision. This makes communication and interpretation visible without adding a new AI platform or monitoring dashboard.

**Alternative more playful activity:** Give pupils an unfamiliar material or simple system and five minutes to discover as many interesting behaviours as possible, without a prescribed final product. Afterwards ask what they noticed, which question appeared next, and whether a tool helped them explore or merely rushed them to an answer. The activity must be adapted to age, subject and classroom needs.

## 15. Possible titles and final questions

**Working title:** *Kellele sa seda teed?* It is intimate and memorable, but may wrongly imply that every creative act needs a recipient.

Other possible questions:

- *Mis juhtub, kui teha saab peaaegu kõike?* (What happens when you can make almost anything?)
- *Kui vastused tulevad kergelt, kust tulevad küsimused?* (When answers come easily, where do questions come from?)
- *Kes kuulab?* (Who is listening?)
- *Kuidas algajast saab asjatundja?* (How does a beginner become an expert?)

The last is the sharpest employment question, but too narrow to carry the entire playful, exploratory talk. The second best connects AI to curiosity, but could sound like a generic education slogan. *Kellele sa seda teed?* remains viable if presented as a question the essay complicates rather than answers.

## 16. Final synthesis

The research does not point to a single easy message. It reveals a set of tensions worth putting on stage:

- AI expands what an individual can make, but making is not the same as learning.
- Play and curiosity can be worthwhile before a purpose is known, but expertise still develops through practice and feedback.
- Solitude can enable exploration, but isolation can deprive makers of response and community.
- New tools may democratise production, but ownership and governance determine how much agency users really have.
- Young people may have more creative power than earlier generations while facing narrower entry routes into some professions.

The keynote should not promise to resolve these contradictions. It should let the audience *experience* one of them through a live creative system, connect it to the real risks young people face, and return to the importance of curiosity and human judgement.

**Possible final thought:**

> We are making it easier to produce answers, programs and entire worlds. But we still have to learn how to ask questions, how to recognise what matters, and how to become capable people among other people.

And then, rather than delivering a prescription:

> *Just the possibility to see what happens next.*
