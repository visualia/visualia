# Second opinion: research update and revised direction

A second agent's report, 2026-11-11, given the brief in `README.md` ("Prompts"). Kept as
written; its claims were not all checked (see `../sources.md`).

**Conference:** Raamistikust klassiruumi
**Date:** 27 November 2026
**Venue:** Öpik Conference Centre, Tallinn
**Format:** 35-minute closing keynote + 15-minute Q&A
**Audience:** Approximately 150 Estonian teachers, school leaders and education technologists
**Research status:** 11 November 2026

## 1. Executive summary

The strongest direction for the keynote is no longer simply that AI allows everyone to
become a software maker.

The more important change is that producing things is becoming easier, while
understanding, judging, sharing and finding meaning in what we produce remain difficult.

The talk should explore the changing relationship between:

- Making something
- Understanding what we have made
- Deciding whether it is worth making
- Working with other people
- Having control over our tools

The speaker's experience with AI-assisted software creation provides the story. Education
provides the larger question.

**Central argument:** When the ability to produce something becomes less scarce, the
human and social conditions that make it meaningful become more important.

This should be explored rather than presented as a proven universal conclusion.

## 2. What has changed in the research

### 2.1 Digital competence frameworks already include creation

The European DigComp framework does not treat people merely as passive technology users.

DigComp 3.0, published in November 2025, integrates AI-related competencies across its
five areas:

1. Information and data literacy
2. Communication and collaboration
3. Digital content creation
4. Safety
5. Problem-solving

Programming, creating digital content and solving problems with technology are already
part of the framework.

**Implication:** Do not criticise HARNO's model for teaching only cautious technology use
without first examining the complete criteria.

The better question is whether pupils experience themselves as people who can shape and
change technology, rather than simply comply with its rules.

Source: European Commission, DigComp 3.0
https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/digcomp_en

### 2.2 Teacher agency is becoming a central issue

The European Commission's work on updating DigCompEdu increasingly addresses educators'
professional judgement and decisions about technology.

This connects directly to the speaker's experimental course design:

- Students run open AI models locally.
- They measure time, energy and cost.
- Students control when inference happens.
- There are no teacher dashboards continuously monitoring their processes.

This is not necessarily an argument against learning analytics.

It is an argument for asking:

- Who decides when technology is used?
- Who controls the information collected?
- Who benefits from that information?
- Can the learner refuse or change the tool?

**Implication:** The keynote can introduce agency and control without becoming an
anti-technology argument.

Source: European Commission, DigCompEdu
https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/digcompedu_en

### 2.3 AI adoption is not the same as educational change

AI Leap has reported high AI adoption among participating Estonian upper-secondary
teachers.

Figures supplied in the research context include:

- 94% of teachers using AI.
- 63% reporting changes to aspects of their teaching.
- A decline in weekly pupil use of a particular application from 47% to 20%.

The exact populations, periods and definitions should be checked before these numbers
appear in the keynote.

The 63% figure is potentially more meaningful than the 94% figure.

Using a tool tells us little about whether teaching or learning has improved.

Changing a teaching practice may be more significant, although self-reported change is
not evidence of improved learning outcomes.

**Implication:** Avoid adoption statistics as measures of success.

Research question: What changed in the classroom, and what evidence do we have that
pupils learned differently?

Source: AI Leap
https://tihupe.ee/

### 2.4 AI changes the value of different kinds of knowledge

Stanford researchers have examined employment changes among young workers in occupations
highly exposed to AI.

The research suggests that workers entering occupations involving codified, easily
documented knowledge may face particular pressure.

Tacit knowledge acquired through experience, judgement, interaction and familiarity with
real situations may be less easily substituted.

The findings should not be simplified into "AI has eliminated 20% of software jobs."

Employment changes have multiple causes, and exposure to AI is not proof of displacement.

**Implication:** The keynote can distinguish between producing an answer and developing
the knowledge needed to judge it.

This connects directly to teaching, where an apparently correct result is not necessarily
evidence of learning.

Source: Stanford Digital Economy Lab
https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/

### 2.5 Technology's value depends on organisation

Estonia's Arenguseire Keskus has examined the economic effects of automation and
organisational change.

The supplied findings indicate:

- Automation alone: estimated productivity improvement of 6–9%.
- Automation combined with organisational changes: larger estimated effects, including
  figures around 19–23% in particular sectors.

These are scenario estimates, not measured universal gains.

The important finding is the relationship between technical capabilities and the
organisation of work.

**Implication:** A more powerful tool does not automatically produce a better outcome.

How people coordinate, learn, make decisions and share knowledge matters.

This is an especially useful bridge between software development and education.

Source: Arenguseire Keskus
https://arenguseire.ee/

## 3. Research and theoretical references

### Seymour Papert: Constructionism

**Reference:** *Mindstorms: Children, Computers, and Powerful Ideas* (1980)

Papert argued that learning can be supported by constructing meaningful objects that
learners can examine, discuss and revise.

This does not mean that making any artefact automatically produces learning.

The process of understanding, testing and reflecting remains essential.

**Relevance:** AI dramatically changes what learners can construct, but does not
eliminate the need for understanding.

### Mitchel Resnick: Creative learning

**Reference:** *Lifelong Kindergarten* (2017)

Resnick's approach emphasises projects, peers, passion and play.

This is a useful corrective to an excessively instrumental argument that children should
only create things useful to others.

**Relevance:** Making things for yourself, experimenting and playing are legitimate forms
of learning.

### Mimi Ito and colleagues: Connected learning

**Reference:** *Connected Learning: An Agenda for Research and Design* (2013)

Connected learning explores relationships between personal interests, peer culture and
meaningful opportunities.

**Relevance:** The lonely-software argument can be developed beyond individual
productivity into participation and relationships.

### Ivan Illich: Convivial tools

**Reference:** *Tools for Conviviality* (1973)

Illich distinguished between tools that support people's autonomy and systems that
constrain how they can act.

His concepts of radical monopoly and learning webs are also relevant.

**Relevance:** Digital competence should include the ability to question, adapt, refuse
and sometimes replace tools.

**Caution:** Illich's work should not be reduced to the claim that open-source software
is automatically convivial.

### Brian Eno: Scenius

Eno's concept of scenius describes creativity emerging from a community rather than a
single exceptional individual.

**Relevance:** The speaker's music background provides a natural way to explain
creativity through listening, responding and improvising.

**Recommendation:** Explain the experience before introducing the term. The word itself
is not essential to the keynote.

### Maggie Appleton: Barefoot developers

Appleton's writing examines the possibility of people building small, personal software
without conventional software-development careers.

Her observation that coding agents are increasingly single-player while software remains
a team sport is particularly relevant.

**Relevance:** AI reduces barriers to making software but does not remove the need for
shared understanding and maintenance.

Source: https://maggieappleton.com/

### Clay Shirky: Situated software

Shirky described software created for specific people and circumstances rather than a
large anonymous market.

**Relevance:** AI may make small, local, temporary and personally meaningful software
more practical.

**Caution:** This possibility does not guarantee accessibility, maintainability or
long-term usefulness.

### Robin Sloan: Home-cooked software

Sloan uses the metaphor of home cooking to describe software created for personal needs
and particular communities.

**Relevance:** Not all software needs to become a product or platform.

Source: https://www.robinsloan.com/

## 4. Estonian references worth developing

### Talgud

Talgud offers a familiar example of collective work involving different abilities and
shared purposes.

It can provide a local connection to the idea of communal creativity.

However, historical talgud were not necessarily egalitarian or voluntary in the modern
sense.

**Possible question:** What would digital talgud look like in a school?

### ProgeTiiger

Estonia's ProgeTiiger initiative provides a local precedent for creative technological
learning.

Investigate examples where pupils create, experiment and collaborate rather than merely
learn to operate software.

**Status:** Specific classroom case study not yet verified.

### Õpilasfirmad

Student companies offer another established Estonian example of learning through making,
testing and responding to other people.

**Status:** A specific example involving AI-assisted creation has not yet been selected
or verified.

### Estonian educational researchers

Potential people to consult:

- Mart Laanpere
- Piret Luik

Ask how the updated digital competence model addresses creation, agency, collaboration
and control over technology.

Do not assume these themes are absent from the framework.

## 5. The strongest objection

A sceptical teacher may argue:

> Children already struggle with reading, reasoning and independent work. AI allows them
> to produce impressive results without understanding anything. Why should we encourage
> more making?

This is a serious objection.

The answer is not that every pupil should become a software developer.

The answer is that producing an artefact and learning something are different activities.

The speaker can acknowledge this distinction from personal experience:

AI has made it possible to produce far more software than before.

But it has not automatically solved the problems of understanding what has been made,
evaluating its quality, or discovering whether it matters.

Teachers already work with this distinction every day.

The keynote should recognise their expertise rather than prescribe a new method.

## 6. Changes recommended for the keynote

### Keep

- The personal history of writing software since 1988.
- The experience of suddenly being able to build entire systems alone.
- The live loopback demonstration.
- The loneliness that can follow creative abundance.
- The question "But who is listening?"
- The open ending from *Lonely Software*.

### Reduce

- Employment-loss statistics.
- Predictions that every pupil will become a programmer.
- Abstract theory and unfamiliar terminology.
- The five-question AI tool assessment framework.
- Claims that human connection is disappearing.
- Instructions telling teachers what they must teach.

### Add

- One concrete example involving a pupil or classroom.
- A distinction between producing and understanding.
- The question of learner and teacher agency.
- A demonstration of what changes when another person participates.
- Recognition that solitary creativity and play have value.
- A practical activity requiring no additional technology.

## 7. Revised narrative structure

### 0–10 minutes: What has changed in making things

Begin with personal experience.

Explain how software creation has changed since 1988.

Demonstrate something that would previously have required substantial time, knowledge or
coordination.

Let the audience experience the excitement of the new capability.

### 10–23 minutes: What has not become easier

Introduce the lonely node and loopback.

Describe the experience of producing more than ever while becoming uncertain about its
purpose.

Explore the difference between generating feedback and receiving meaningful responses
from another person.

Connect this to understanding, judgement and learning.

### 23–32 minutes: Tools and participation

Describe the local AI course.

Explain why students can control when inference occurs and why continuous monitoring is
excluded.

Explore the difference between operating a platform and having agency over a tool.

Connect to collective creativity, music and possible Estonian examples.

### 32–35 minutes: Return to the listener

Return to the original demonstration.

Ask what changes when another person enters the process.

Offer one classroom example.

Finish with an open question rather than a demand.

## 8. One practical classroom activity

### Someone else tries it

**Time:** Approximately 10 minutes
**Technology required:** None

Students take something they have already created.

It might be a drawing, explanation, story, mathematical solution, presentation or small
program.

They give it to another pupil without explaining it first.

The other pupil answers:

1. What did you understand?
2. Where did you need help, or what would you change?

The creator then decides whether to revise the work and explains why.

The purpose is to experience the difference between producing something and
communicating meaning.

The activity is relevant to AI-assisted creation but does not depend on AI.

## 9. Claims requiring further verification

Before the keynote, verify:

- Pipedrive's October 2026 layoffs, exact number and stated causes.
- OSKA's figures for under-25 IT employment.
- The precise Stanford employment estimates and comparison groups.
- AI Leap's reported changes in teacher and pupil AI use.
- The approximately 60 AI-related criteria in Estonia's updated model.
- Whether the model already addresses agency, making and collaboration.
- Specific Estonian examples of student-led technological creation.
- The wording and status of the proposed DigCompEdu update.

Avoid presenting company statements about AI-related restructuring as independent proof
of AI-driven unemployment.

## 10. Final direction

The keynote should not argue that teachers must turn pupils into software makers.

It should explore what happens when making things becomes dramatically easier.

The speaker can demonstrate this change directly.

The audience can bring its expertise in learning, development, judgement and
relationships.

The meeting point is the difference between producing something and understanding why
it matters.

The strongest question to leave with the audience is:

**Now that we can make so much on our own, what do we still need one another for?**

This is consistent with the speaker's essay *Lonely Software*, the conference's focus on
purposeful technology use, and the educational importance of human relationships.

It remains a question worth exploring, not a conclusion that technology has already
answered.
