# Value drift and teaching

Draft 2026-11-02, from a claude.ai chat (24 October to 2 November). What value
drift means for an art school, who the MA students are, how critique works,
and where recording students' process must stop. The platform rule that
follows is `plans/backstage.md`. The conference demo discussed in the same
chat (the "Drift Machine") was dropped.

## Value drift

### The Estonian framing

- **TI-Hüpe risk analysis** (University of Tartu Centre for Ethics, commissioned
  by SA TI-Hüpe, August 2026, published mid-September): 12 risks in three
  areas (educational, societal, technology and governance).
  - **Risk 6, Estonian culture:** the learning app may not be grounded enough
    in the Estonian curriculum, Estonian-language resources and local cultural
    context, threatening the sustainability of Estonian language and culture.
  - **Risk 7, value drift:** an AI learning app controlled by an external
    service provider may mediate values that conflict with the fundamental
    values of the Estonian state.
  - **Risk 12, domestic solution:** a home-built app may be lower in quality
    and security than a foreign cloud service.
  - Main conclusion (Margit Sutrop): the biggest risks come from how the
    technology is used and from educational and political choices, not from
    the technology itself. AI is not value-neutral; it can seem an empathetic,
    supportive partner.
- **Sirp, "Maailmalõpp ja „normaalne" tehisaru"** (October 2026): lists value
  drift among risks already happening (with deepfakes, bias, information
  pollution, mass surveillance, concentration of capital, environmental harm,
  devaluation of work), as opposed to existential scenarios.
- **Earlier TI-Hüpe debate (ERR, 2025):** Tragel and Hint (students and
  teachers can't control AI tools' content), Oliver Laas (AI ethics), Kersna
  and Laak (skills AI itself doesn't develop).
- **Language and archives:** the DIGAR dispute over locked media archives and
  data for Estonian-language AI (Pakosta; Öövel and Kruusmaa).

### The wider meaning for creative fields

Value drift: a slow, unchosen shift in what is considered good, important or
normal as AI mediates more of daily life. No single step is a decision; the
sum is.

1. **Aesthetic drift** toward the average and the describable.
2. **Process drift:** speed over making; friction seen as inefficiency.
3. **Authorship drift:** from making to selecting.
4. **Judgement drift:** what counts becomes what's measurable.
5. **Cultural drift:** what isn't in the data fades (small languages,
   dialects, vernacular forms, local craft).
6. **Labour:** creative work seen as cheap content.
7. **Education's own drift:** feedback tools sliding into monitoring.

Mechanism: feedback loops (defaults accepted, AI output re-enters culture and
training data), no moment of choice, hard to see from inside.

What an art school adds to TI-Hüpe's sovereignty framing: drift is also in
what AI *makes* (aesthetic, cultural, process), and not only from foreign
providers; our own well-meant tools can drift too.

## Teaching and its boundaries

### The students

MA programmes in the Faculty of Design (taught in English, largely
international): Graphic Design, Interaction Design, Craft Studies, Service
Design (SDSI, Erasmus Mundus with the Art Academy of Latvia and the University
of Lapland), Circular Design, Design & Technology Futures (with TalTech, if
running); plus students from other faculties.

| MA | Brings | Fits |
|---|---|---|
| Graphic Design | typography, ephemera | anti-skill |
| Interaction Design | interfaces, gestures | board, CIPHER + Clef |
| Craft Studies | glass, ceramics, metal, textile surfaces | texture NCA, small GAN |
| Service Design | processes, interviews | Judgement Machines over processes |
| Circular Design | material and energy | usage and Wh logs |
| Design & Technology Futures | prototyping | pool, Burn |

International students bring their own vernacular cultures (anti-drift
without exoticising others); Estonian pre-war material is a shared local
layer; captions in students' own languages; small-data work can feed theses.

### Critique

Donald Schön (*The Reflective Practitioner*, 1983; *Educating the Reflective
Practitioner*, 1987): design as a reflective conversation with materials; the
crit makes tacit decisions discussable. Formats: process crit, counterfactual
crit (branches), pinned peer crit (threads, not shapes), mirror crit (students
see their own patterns and agree or disagree), Judgement Machine crit (AI as
one voice, interesting where confidently wrong), class pattern crit. The AI
asks questions rather than judging, since VLMs are weak design judges.

### The boundary: no "big design brother"

The decision-record idea drifted, step by step, into a surveillance system for
creative work: exactly the value drift described above. The problem
isn't logging (Figma, Docs and git already keep everything) but:

- **The flow:** Helen Nissenbaum's contextual integrity: the same version
  history is fine for you and collaborators, different when it flows to the
  person who grades you.
- **Interpretation:** inferred rules and taste models are a profile, a claim
  about who you are.
- **The backstage:** Erving Goffman's front and back stage. Exploration needs
  a safe place for bad ideas; an analysed backstage invites performance and
  narrows exploration (another route to homogenization). Being reduced to
  "your rules" is the same flattening we criticise in the models.

Principles:

- Logging can stay (undo, versions, recovery).
- Inference for the student, by the student, only on request.
- The backstage private by default; publishing to the front stage is an
  explicit step.
- Nothing interpretive kept unless the student keeps it.
- No teacher dashboard of process, no automatic grading of process, no class
  comparisons of process; only works students choose to show.

Untouched by any tracking: small-data training, Judgement
Machines on submitted works, the anti-skill, archive work. The boundary itself
is course material: let students design what may be recorded, by whom, for
whom.
